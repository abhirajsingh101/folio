The Note at the Foot of the Page

Scholarship kept a device that interrupts reading on purpose. What the interruption buys, and what it costs to set.

AuthorH. M. Ardley
SeriesEssays on the book
Date2026.08.15
Reading12 minutes

The footnote is the only part of a book that lets a reader check the author without leaving the page. It sits under a rule, in smaller type, on the same sheet as the sentence that provoked it, and the reader spends a glance rather than a search deciding whether to take it up. That geometry is the entire argument for the device, and it is the first thing every modern substitute gives away.

The claim of this essay is narrow: the position of a note is not a formatting preference, it is part of what the note can do. Move a note to the back of the book and it does not become a shorter note or a quieter one — it becomes a note most readers will never read, which is a different piece of writing altogether. The case rests on two things a reader can check. The history is documented, and the reasons notes migrated to the end are production reasons rather than scholarly ones. The mechanics are harder than they look, and that difficulty explains why so much of what we read now has no footnotes in it at all.

01A device with a date

The footnote feels like a natural property of scholarship, the way the paragraph feels like a natural property of prose. It is not. It was invented, argued over, ridiculed and eventually standardised, mostly between the seventeenth and the nineteenth centuries, and Anthony Grafton’s history of it is a history of a profession learning to show its working.Anthony Grafton, The Footnote: A Curious History (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1997); first published in German in 1995. Grafton’s subject is the note as evidence of method rather than as a citation format. Before the apparatus, a scholar’s authority was the scholar. After it, the authority sat on the page in small type, where a rival could reach it.

That is an unusual thing for a piece of typography to accomplish, and it is why the device survived every attempt to be rid of it. A footnote is a standing invitation to be contradicted, made in the one place where the reader is still holding the sentence that provoked it. An author who cites in the back of the book makes the same promise at a distance that makes it much harder to collect on.

Apparatus

Everything on the page that is not the argument: notes, sigla, variant readings, the record of what a text said before an editor touched it. Set small, and read first by whoever came to check.

The ridicule arrived with the device and never left.Chuck Zerby, The Devil’s Details: A History of Footnotes (Montpelier, VT: Invisible Cities Press, 2002), a defence of the note written against exactly this tradition of mockery. Reading a footnote has been likened to going downstairs to answer the door in the middle of making love — a line usually given to Noël Coward, sometimes to John Barrymore, and to my knowledge never traced to a printed source by either.The attribution is unstable in precisely the way a footnote exists to prevent, which is the only reason it is quoted here. Anyone holding a citation for it has a small piece of scholarship to publish.

02What the foot of the page does

The distance between a call and its note is the whole user interface of the device, and the units are not millimetres but decisions. At the foot of the page the cost is a glance: the eye drops, reads six or eight lines of smaller type, and comes back to a sentence it never let go of. At the end of the chapter it is a thumb and a search. At the end of the book it is a hand held in one place, a second search through a numbered list, and a return journey — which is to say it is a task, and tasks are declined.

Publishers know this, and the manuals say so plainly rather than pretending otherwise: notes at the foot are easier on the reader, notes at the back are easier on the book.See The Chicago Manual of Style, 17th ed. (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2017), which documents both systems and is candid that the choice turns on production as much as on the reader’s convenience. Endnotes make a page cheaper to set, let a compositor break wherever the text falls, and keep the type area clean for a designer who would rather not think about a variable-height block appearing at the bottom of an unknown page. Every one of those is a real advantage. Not one of them is an argument about reading.

A note the reader will not turn to is not a shorter note. It is a deleted one.— the case against endnotes, in one sentence

03The second voice

Gérard Genette put notes among the paratext: the apparatus of thresholds — titles, prefaces, epigraphs, notes — through which a text is presented and by which it is framed before anyone reads a word of the thing itself.Gérard Genette, Paratexts: Thresholds of Interpretation, trans. Jane E. Lewin (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1997); originally Seuils (Paris: Éditions du Seuil, 1987). The description is exact, and it explains what the foot of the page is for. A note is a second voice, belonging to the same author, speaking at a different volume about the same sentence. It is where the qualification goes that would wreck the sentence if it were admitted into it.

This is why a confident prose style and a heavy apparatus are not in tension, as they are usually assumed to be. They are the same decision. An argument can afford to state itself cleanly precisely because the exception, the source, the case that does not fit and the debt to whoever said it first are all available six lines below without entering the clause. Strip the notes away and one of two things happens: the prose fills with hedging, or the qualifications quietly go missing. Both are worse, and the second is dishonest.

The typographic discipline is the same discipline. A column is set between about forty-five and seventy-five characters because past that the eye loses the line on the return.Robert Bringhurst, The Elements of Typographic Style, 4th ed. (Point Roberts, WA: Hartley & Marks, 2012), whose measure is the source of the range this page is set to. The same reasoning governs a note: what matters is not that the reader can reach it but how far the eye must travel and whether the journey is refusable. A note at the foot of the page can be ignored at no cost, which is exactly why it can be taken up at no cost either. Both halves of that come from the same geometry.

04Why so little of it is set

There is a mechanical reason the footnote is rare outside books, and it is not laziness. A footnote creates a circular dependency in the one part of typesetting that has no easy solutions: what sits on a page determines where the page breaks, and where the page breaks determines which notes belong on it. Add a note and the text moves up; move the text and a call may cross onto the next page, taking its note — and the space the note occupied — with it.

The problem is worse than it sounds. Michael Plass’s thesis, written alongside the work that produced TeX, showed that pagination with floating material is NP-complete: there is no efficient algorithm that lays out a document with figures and notes optimally, and no single pass that gets it right.Michael F. Plass, “Optimal Pagination Techniques for Automatic Typesetting Systems” (PhD diss., Stanford University, 1981). The easier problem, where a global optimum is reachable, is Donald E. Knuth and Michael F. Plass, “Breaking Paragraphs into Lines,” Software: Practice and Experience 11, no. 11 (1981): 1119–84. TeX’s answer was to give notes a mechanism of their own: material contributed to the page as it fills rather than placed in the stream, so a note travels with the line that called it.Donald E. Knuth, The TeXbook (Reading, MA: Addison-Wesley, 1984), ch. 15, on how TeX makes lines into pages. Inserts are the machinery footnotes ride on. Forty years on, that is still roughly how every engine that sets a real footnote does it.

The web never had pages, so it never had the problem and never solved it. CSS eventually specified the behaviour — a note declared inline, floated to the foot of whatever page it lands on, numbered by the renderer’s own counterThe property is float: footnote, from the W3C’s CSS Generated Content for Paged Media module. Every implementation is a print engine; no shipping browser has one, because no browser paginates. — but the implementations live in print engines that most people never touch. So on screen the note became a hyperlink, then a tooltip, then a panel that slides in from the side. Each of those replaced a glance with a click, and a click is a decision made before you can see what is on the other side of it. That is precisely the cost the footnote was invented to remove.

05What would change my mind

The foot of the page is a means, not a principle. The principle is that a note must be reachable in a glance and refusable at no cost, and if something else delivered both, the position would stop mattering. A sidenote in a permanently wide outer margin does deliver both, and on a large enough sheet it is the better device — Tschichold’s margins were generous enough to hold one, and the reader never leaves the line at all.Jan Tschichold, The Form of the Book: Essays on the Morality of Good Design, trans. Hajo Hadeler (Point Roberts, WA: Hartley & Marks, 1991), on page proportion and the uses of a wide margin. A reflowable screen that kept notes in view beside the text, without displacing it and without asking anything of the reader, would satisfy the argument completely, and I would drop the preference for the foot of the page the day one existed.

What would not satisfy it is anything that trades the glance for a decision, however small the decision looks in a usability report. The distinction is not between paper and screen, and treating it that way is how the argument usually gets lost. It is between a device the eye can decline mid-sentence and a device the hand must commit to.

The practical consequence for anyone setting a document is smaller than the argument and worth stating anyway. If a piece has fewer than a dozen notes and they are commentary rather than citation, set them at the foot of the page and accept the pagination cost, because commentary that is not read is not commentary. If it has four hundred notes and they are pure citation — page numbers, shelf marks, ibid. — put them at the back, where they belong to the record rather than to the reading. The mistake is not choosing endnotes. The mistake is choosing them for a piece whose notes were arguments.

06A note on this document

Set in P052, a metric clone of Hermann Zapf’s Palatino, at 10.2 point over 15.5, with labels and running heads in Inter. The measure is 130 millimetres, inside the range cited in section 03. The sheet is A4, bound: 26 millimetres at the spine against 24 at the fore-edge, the difference being what a binding takes. The running heads swap sides with the margin, so a reader riffling the right-hand edge sees the section rather than the title of the book they are already holding.

The ten notes were authored inline, in the sentences that provoked them, and placed by the renderer: no number here was typed by its author, and no note was assigned to a page by hand. It is the one claim in this document a reader can check without leaving it — read a note, find its call, and see whether the two are ever on different sheets.
