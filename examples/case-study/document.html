01The situation

Twenty-seven of Meridian’s forty-three services were already off the monolith. The programme had stalled anyway, because everything left shared one orders schema, and every change to it needed a window that three teams had to agree on.

By September those windows were holding eleven releases a month — one in three. The cost was not the waiting: changes were batched to fit a window, so each release carried more and its failures were harder to attribute. Peak season started on 24 November and a freeze was not available.

Meridian’s platform team knew what needed doing and could not staff it alongside the remaining sixteen service moves. The brief was that sentence: take the schema, leave the services.

02What we did

The extraction itself was unremarkable, which was the point. The nine tables with writers outside the order path went behind an API first, with shadow traffic compared for seventy-two hours before anything was believed. Then dual-write, and a reconciliation report a human could read every morning — not a dashboard, a list of divergences with the row attached.

The decision that mattered was making the cutover reversible for a fortnight rather than a single night. It cost two weeks of dual-write, and it meant nobody had to be brave at three in the morning: the switch was two config changes, the rollback the same two in reverse, timed at four minutes in rehearsal.

What went wrong

Two things, both in discovery. The schema export we priced against was a week old, so there were forty-three tables by the start, not forty-one — small, but we quoted the change rather than absorbing it, which is the habit that keeps the last invoice boring. The second rehearsal found the writer queue took 140 seconds to drain, not 90; the procedure said 90 before it was ever used in anger, and says 180 now.

03Where it left them

Meridian ran the cutover themselves, from a runbook they had rehearsed unaided — the deliverable that decides whether an engagement leaves anything behind. Thirty-four minutes, no downtime, and the old instance retired three weeks later on their own schedule.

Releases held for a schema window went from eleven in September to one in February, and that one was a coordination rather than a queue. The sixteen remaining service moves are back with the platform team, unblocked, and there is no standing dependency on us: the runbook is theirs to revise, and we are not on the rota.

We’d been told before that a migration would be zero-downtime. This is the first time someone showed us the rollback and made us run it ourselves. — Principal engineer, Meridian Platform Engineering
