The claim, not the topic

A subtitle earns its place by narrowing the claim — the case it holds for, or the one it does not.

Open on the claim itself. Not the background, not the history of the field, and not an apology for the length: the sentence a reader would repeat to someone else if they only read one. Everything after this earns its place by supporting it.

Then the ground the claim stands on. An essay is not a report — it argues rather than records — but the argument still rests on something a reader can check, and naming it early is what separates an essay from an opinion.A footnote: authored here, in the sentence that provoked it, and set at the foot of whatever page this sentence lands on. Never type the number — the call and the marker are the renderer’s counter. Delete this note if the piece cites nothing. Say where the evidence comes from and how far it reaches.

01What is actually happening

One idea per section, and the section heading is the idea rather than a label for a bucket. “What is actually happening” is a claim; “Background” is furniture. If a heading could sit unchanged in any other essay, it is not doing any work.

The rail beside this paragraph is for the aside that would otherwise derail the sentence — a definition, a date, a caveat that matters to one reader in ten. Prose that keeps interrupting itself is prose that has not decided what it is about.

Keep the main column moving. A reader who is following an argument forgives a long paragraph and never forgives a digression, because the digression costs them the thread they were holding.

Worth knowing

The one fact a reader needs to follow the paragraph beside this, and would resent being taught in the middle of it.

02Why the obvious reading is wrong

The section that makes the piece worth reading. State the reading a sensible person would arrive at on their own, as strongly as they would state it — an essay that argues against a weak version of the other side convinces only the people who already agreed.

The sentence you would want quoted back at you.— pull it from the prose, do not write it for the box

Then the turn: what that reading misses, and what follows once it is corrected. This is the paragraph the whole document exists to deliver, so give it room — it should be the one place where you slow down rather than compress.

03What it costs to believe this

Every claim worth making has a price: something it rules out, someone it inconveniences, a case where it fails. Naming that yourself is not a concession — it is the difference between an argument and a pitch, and a reader who finds the exception you did not mention stops trusting the parts they cannot check.

04What would change my mind

Name the evidence that would make you drop the claim — the measurement that would come out the other way, the case you have not seen yet. It is the most persuasive paragraph in any argument, and the one most often left out, because it is the only proof on offer that the conclusion was not decided in advance.

Close on what changes if the reader accepts it. Not a summary: they have just read the essay, and repeating it back to them wastes the one paragraph they are most likely to remember.

The author

Who wrote it, when, and what it draws on — a version of the argument given as a talk, a longer paper it condenses, the work that paid for the time. Say how to reply, too: the useful response to an argument is a counter-argument, and a reader who has one needs somewhere to send it.
